01

Nihonbashi

Every Tōkaidō journey began here, at the bridge from which road distances were measured. Hiroshige shows it at dawn: a lord's procession tops the arch, feathered spears bobbing, while fish sellers with shoulder baskets jostle in the foreground. Look to the right and a stray dog trots in, the first of the road's many unbothered locals.

Today the stone bridge of 1911 sits in the shade of an expressway, watched over by winged bronze beasts. Our porter pushes a hand truck stacked with parcels, office workers hurry past, and a dog still crosses as if it owns the place. Keep Hiroshige's indigo for the sky.

Then Lords and fishmongers crowding the bridge at dawn.

Now Parcels rolling under the expressway at rush hour.

Shinagawa

Shinagawa was the first stop out of Edo, and Hiroshige catches the moment of leaving: the tail of a lord's procession filing past seaside teahouses at sunrise, while sails crowd the bay. Friends often walked travelers this far before saying goodbye, so the pink morning sky carries a small ache.

The shoreline has moved far out, but lantern-hung pleasure boats still moor along the old waterway, and towers rise beyond them. Our procession is a courier shouldering boxes up the lane, a cyclist, a woman checking her phone, someone tying up at the quay. Departures, it turns out, never stop.

Then A lord's retinue leaving Edo as sails fill the bay.

Now A courier climbing the lane past lantern-hung boats.

03

Kawasaki

For most of the Edo period there was no bridge here. Floods kept washing out the Rokugo bridge, so travelers crossed the Tama River by ferry, just as Hiroshige shows: a flat boat poled across the current, a laden horse waiting on the far bank, and Fuji floating pale on the horizon.

Now an arched bridge carries commuter trains overhead, planes climb from the nearby airport, and Fuji still sits small and patient in the distance. Our ferry party has traded straw hats for life jackets and paddles its own little boat, a small dog along for the ride. Color the river indigo and leave the snowcap white.

Then A ferry poled across the Tama River toward Fuji.

Now Life jackets and paddles under the railway arch.

04

Kanagawa

On the hill road above the bay, Kanagawa's teahouses stood shoulder to shoulder, and their staff did not wait to be noticed. In Hiroshige's print, women step out to tug passing travelers toward their doors, while ships ride at anchor in the blue water below. It was a pleasant place to rest, and a hard one to leave.

That quiet bay became the port of Yokohama, marked now by a Ferris wheel and towers across the water. On our slope the tradition holds: a café worker with a tray of free tastings catches a hiker by the backpack, while a courier wheels boxes uphill. Try vermilion for the awning.

Then Teahouse maids pulling travelers in by the sleeve.

Now Free samples stopping hikers by the backpack.

Hodogaya

Hodogaya begins with a bridge. In Hiroshige's print, palanquin bearers trot across the curved Shinmachi Bridge over the Katabira River, and thatched shops wait on the far side beneath a sky banded with orange. The road west starts climbing soon after, so this was the place to catch one's breath before the hills.

The river still runs through town, and trains now glide along a viaduct above the rooftops. On our bridge the bearers have become a delivery worker pushing a cart of boxes, with neighbors strolling ahead toward the shops under a low autumn sun. A little ochre and persimmon orange will warm the whole scene.

Then Palanquin bearers jogging over the river bridge.

Totsuka

At Totsuka a traveler swings down from his horse outside the Komeya teahouse, and Hiroshige freezes him mid-dismount, one foot reaching for the bench. Paper notices hang from the eaves, and a stone post points the side road toward Kamakura. Many travelers from Edo spent their first night here, a long day's walk from Nihonbashi.

Our rider arrives by bus and hops off the step just as hastily, while a traffic guide waves a light baton and a passerby clutches a hat against the breeze. The stone lantern and the roadside marker are still standing. Vehicles change; impatience does not. A bright plaid will suit the jumper.

Fujisawa

Fujisawa grew up around Yugyoji temple, whose roofs rise from the trees in Hiroshige's print. In the foreground, beneath a great torii, a line of blind men with staffs makes its way toward the bridge. The gate marked the road to Enoshima, the island shrine of Benzaiten, goddess of music and a patron of blind performers.

Enoshima still draws pilgrims, though many now come for the waves. Our cyclist pedals under the gate with a surfboard clipped to the bike, past a child in a sun hat and a neighbor in a flowered coat, while the temple steps climb behind. Summer blue for the river, vermilion for the bridge.

Then Blind pilgrims tapping toward the island shrine.

Now Surfboards on bike racks bound for the same shore.

Hiratsuka

Along the raised causeway through the rice fields, bearers jog home with an empty palanquin while a lone porter hurries the other way. Behind them rises the round green bulk of Mount Koma, with Fuji peeking over its shoulder. Hiroshige makes the flat land feel fast.

Every January the road sees a different kind of runner, because Hiratsuka is a relay point of the Hakone Ekiden, the great college relay between Tokyo and Hakone. Our runner sprints down the country road with a camera bike close behind, as a volunteer sets out cups of water. The hill and Fuji watch it all, unhurried as ever.

Then Bearers jogging the causeway with an empty palanquin.

Now A relay runner racing the camera bike to the handoff.

Oiso

Rain slants across the whole of Hiroshige's Oiso, and the title explains why: Tora's Rain. Legend says the showers of the twenty-eighth day of the fifth month are the tears of Tora, a woman of Oiso mourning her lover, Soga Juro. Travelers in straw capes bend beneath the pines toward the inn roofs.

The coast road through Oiso is still lined with old pines, and the sea still shows gray past them. In our version the straw capes have become rain jackets and a dotted umbrella, and someone pushes a stroller under its rain cover past pots of flowers. Let sumi and blue-gray carry this wet page.

Then Straw rain capes bowed under Tora's tears.

Now A covered stroller splashing down the pine-lined walk.

Odawara

Before reaching Odawara's castle town, travelers had to cross the Sakawa River, and in Hiroshige's print they do it on other people's shoulders. Porters wade the current carrying a palanquin on a platform, while the castle rises small against the Hakone mountains beyond. Here travelers rested before the hardest climb on the whole road.

Today the Sakawa is a favorite river for ayu, the sweetfish of Japanese summers, and anglers stand thigh-deep where porters once waded. Our scene follows a fisherman down the embankment steps, rod on his shoulder, past an older companion and a child with a fish on the backpack. The castle still keeps watch from its hill.

Then Porters wading the Sakawa with a palanquin aloft.

Now Anglers wading the same river for sweetfish.

Hakone

Hiroshige never drew a stranger mountain. In his Hakone, the pass rises as a jagged mosaic of green, ochre and slate blue, and a tiny procession creeps along its foot, dwarfed by the rock. Lake Ashi lies calm to the left, Fuji white beyond. Ahead waited the Hakone checkpoint and its inspectors.

Hakone is now a holiday playground, and the lake carries sightseeing boats dressed up as old sailing ships. Our climbers scramble over the same faceted rocks with trekking poles and backpacks, one pausing for a drink, while a cyclist grinds up the road above. Borrow Hiroshige's patchwork palette and give each rock face its own color.

Then A tiny procession inching under the jagged peaks.

Mishima

Mishima appears out of the fog. Hiroshige prints it in soft layers of gray, with the torii of Mishima Shrine and the far houses fading like ghosts, while a palanquin party and a rider on horseback press forward in full color. Coming down from Hakone, travelers found level ground here at last.

The shrine still stands on the old road, and the town is known for clear spring water that wells up from Fuji's snowmelt. Our travelers wait at a crosswalk: a hiker with trekking poles, a neighbor with a shopping cart, a courier on a scooter loaded with boxes. Soft grays will bring back the mist.

Numazu

Twilight at Numazu, with a full moon rising behind the trees along the Kano River. In front walks a pilgrim with a huge red-faced tengu mask strapped to his back, an offering bound for the Konpira shrine, followed by a mother and daughter. It is the quietest of walks home.

The river still curves through Numazu, now with a lamp-lined path along its bank. Our trio heads home in the same order: a teenager with a guitar case where the tengu mask used to ride, a woman with a shopping bag, a small child with a backpack. Try deep evening indigo and a pale, glowing moon.

Hara

At Hara, Fuji is too big for the picture. Hiroshige lets its peak break right through the top border, while two women and their servant walk the path through the marshy fields and a crane feeds among the reeds. The post town sat on low wetland with nothing to block the view.

Our Hara keeps the giant mountain and the cranes, now gliding over young rice. A bullet train slides along its viaduct, a farmer works his cart down the lane past a vending machine, and a mother pedals by with a child in the back seat. Leave Fuji's slopes pale; a single band of blue sky will do.

Yoshiwara

Fuji usually rose on a westbound traveler's right, but near Yoshiwara the road bends, and for a short stretch it appears on the left. Hiroshige frames that famous left-hand Fuji between pine trunks and adds a small delight: three children riding together on one horse, perched in a saddle frame while the groom leads them on.

Fuji City, which absorbed old Yoshiwara, is known today for its paper mills. Our three young riders are toddlers in a nursery outing cart, pushed by a caregiver along a pine-lined road, with factory chimneys puffing in the distance and Fuji, sure enough, on the left. Pick a cheerful flower color for the cart.

Then Three children riding one horse under the pines.

16

Kambara

Hiroshige's Kambara is one of the most admired snow scenes in Japanese art: a sleeping village under deep white, flakes drifting from a dark sky, two figures trudging uphill and one heading down. The irony is that this mild stretch of coast seldom sees snow like this. Imagined or not, the silence is perfect.

Our snowy night keeps the slope and the hush. A walker heads down under a clear umbrella, shopping bag in hand, while a father in a knit cap climbs the hill pulling a child on a sled. Snow caps the lamp posts, the traffic mirror, even the parked car. Keep most of the page white.

Then Lone walkers bent under a silent night snowfall.

Now An umbrella going down, a sled going up.

Yui

From the Satta Pass, Hiroshige gives travelers their reward for a nerve-racking cliff path: Fuji and the blue curve of Suruga Bay, with sails scattered below. Look at the upper left and you will find tiny figures peering over the edge to take it in, the first sightseers of this famous view.

Below the lookout, the expressway, the national highway and the railway now squeeze side by side along the narrow shore, while container ships cross the bay. Our sightseers point and shoot with a long lens as a farmer climbs past with a basket. Give the bay Hiroshige's deep blue.

Okitsu

Okitsu holds one of the series' best jokes. Two sumo wrestlers are crossing the Okitsu River, one on a straining horse, the other crammed into a palanquin borne by porters who look ready to sink. Behind them a red rock rises beside a calm sea. Hiroshige plainly enjoyed the gag.

The hills around Okitsu are mandarin country, and a well-known early satsuma variety even carries the town's name. Our heavy load rides on a small truck piled high with crates of fruit, while pickers follow in boots with buckets on their shoulders, and a container ship slips past in the harbor beyond. Save your brightest orange for this page.

Then Sumo wrestlers carried over the river by groaning porters.

Now A small truck groaning under crates of mandarins.

Ejiri

Ejiri is seen from high above: a harbor thick with masts, white sails gliding over the bay, and the long arm of the Miho peninsula curling around the water. It is one of Hiroshige's calmest views. Ejiri was a busy port town, and Miho's pine-lined beach was already celebrated in poetry and legend.

Ejiri now belongs to the port of Shimizu, and a small water bus still crosses the bay toward Miho, whose pine grove is part of the Fuji World Heritage listing. Our travelers stroll the pier toward the boat, a cyclist walking a bike beside them, past fishing boats and iron bollards. Paint the sea a soft aqua.

Then Sails crowding the harbor below the Miho pines.

Now A water bus waiting for bikes and day-trippers.

Fuchu

Fuchu, the old Sunpu, was the castle town where Tokugawa Ieyasu spent his retirement, and travelers leaving it soon met the Abe River. With no bridge, they crossed as Hiroshige shows: porters wading through the current, a woman riding on a platform carried high, and a pack horse led through the water.

Today broad bridges span the river, so the shallows belong to families. A father wades in with his daughter on his shoulders, just as the porters once carried their passengers, while his son chases fish with a net and Grandma keeps watch from the shade of the tent. Kinako tan for the hats, and a clear summer blue for the water.

Then Porters carry travelers across on their shoulders.

Mariko

In Mariko, Hiroshige stops for lunch. Two travelers sit at a thatched teahouse as a woman serves them, and the sign out front advertises the local specialty, tororo-jiru: grated mountain yam poured over rice. The plum tree beside the roof is just coming into bloom, so it must be early spring.

A thatched restaurant beside the bridge in Mariko has been serving tororo-jiru since 1596, and still does. Our view looks out from a tatami room: a diner waits with his bowl as a server brings a wooden tub of barley rice, and outside someone strolls by with a child on her back. Plum pink, please.

Then Travelers slurping yam soup at a roadside teahouse.

Okabe

The Utsunoya Pass was dark and narrow, and Hiroshige makes it feel tighter still: steep green walls close in on both sides, a stream rushes beside the path, and villagers climb with great bundles of brushwood on their backs. A single tree in autumn red glows near the top like a lantern.

The old valley path is now a walking route, and the pass has been tunneled again and again; the first tunnel, opened in 1876, was the first in Japan to charge a toll. Our hikers climb toward a tunnel mouth past stacked firewood and a parked van. Moss green and stone gray suit this page.

Then Brushwood bundles carried up a dark mountain gorge.

Now Day hikers climbing toward a tunnel mouth.

Fujieda

Every post town kept a relay office where fresh porters and horses took over the loads, and Hiroshige's Fujieda shows the handover in full swing. Porters wipe their sweat and stretch their backs, a horse is unloaded, and officials at the doorway check the paperwork. It is the shogun's freight system as a crowd scene.

The baton has passed to the delivery depot. In our year-end scene, workers in reflective jackets heave boxes from a truck onto the loading dock, one pushing a hand truck while another sits for a breather, and vans wait in the yard. The job is unchanged: keep things moving.

Then Fresh porters and horses taking over the loads.

Now Boxes passed from truck to van in the year-end rush.

Shimada

The Oi River was the great obstacle of the Tōkaidō. There was no bridge and no ferry, only professional porters who carried travelers across, and when the water ran high everyone waited, sometimes for days. Hiroshige looks down on the wide riverbed at Shimada as travelers, horses and porters gather on the sand.

The river is bridged now, and its broad gravel banks have become playing fields. Our scene looks down from the levee on a Sunday morning youth soccer match, with tents and parked cars at the edge and a family carrying a cooler down the steps. The riverbed crowd has simply found a new reason to gather.

Then Crowds waiting on the sand for porters to carry them.

Now Families on the levee cheering a youth soccer match.

Kanaya

Kanaya is the far side of the same crossing. Having made it over the Oi River, Hiroshige's travelers come ashore beneath green hills, with palanquins still riding on porters' shoulders through the shallows behind them. From here the road climbed straight back into hill country.

When the river crossing system ended in 1870, the plateau above Kanaya was being cleared for tea, and some of the jobless porters found work there. Makinohara is now one of Japan's great tea districts. Our pickers carry fresh leaves in spring while a harvester trims the rows and frost fans stand guard. Fresh green, of course.

Then Porters hauling palanquins up the far riverbank.

Now Pickers hauling new tea leaves along the rows.

Nissaka

Between Kanaya and Nissaka lay the Sayo no Nakayama pass, so steep that Hiroshige's road seems to tilt off the page. In the middle of the path sits a round boulder: the Night-Weeping Stone, which, legend says, cried out after a pregnant woman was murdered on the pass, so that her baby was found and saved.

The slope is still a real climb. Our travelers are high school students walking home, one pushing a bicycle uphill, with the round stone resting at the edge of the road and an expressway bridge crossing the valley far below. Late afternoon suits it; try a soft orange sky over blue-gray hills.

Then Travelers toiling past the stone that wept at night.

Now Students pushing bikes up the same steep pass.

Kakegawa

On the Oike Bridge outside Kakegawa, travelers bow politely to a passing priest, while a child gapes after a kite whose string has snapped and which is sailing away above the rice fields. Mount Akiba rises in the distance; its shrine, famed for protection against fire, drew pilgrims up a side road from this town.

Our bridge is busy with the same courtesies. A priest on a scooter stops to return the bows of two passersby, a child flies a kite with a bold crest, and a tractor works the field beyond. Winter sky, bright kite: a little vermilion goes a long way here.

Then Bows on the bridge as a runaway kite sails off.

Fukuroi

Fukuroi sits right in the middle of the fifty-three stations, and Hiroshige gives it the simplest of scenes: a roadside tea stall under a great tree, a kettle hanging from a branch over the fire, travelers taking a breather. Find the small bird perched on the signboard at right, quite unbothered.

Today Fukuroi is home to a large stadium, and its open-air tea stall has become a roasted sweet potato truck with smoke curling from its stove. A traveler rests on the bench, a delivery man leans in to order, and the bird is still on its sign. Warm ochre for the smoke.

Then A kettle steaming under the tree at the halfway mark.

Now Sweet potatoes roasting on a truck at the halfway mark.

Mitsuke

Between Mitsuke and Hamamatsu ran the wide Tenryu River, and travelers crossed it by ferry. In Hiroshige's misty scene, two boatmen wait in their beached boat, one standing by his long pole, the other crouched beside him. Look past them to the sandbar, where people are trudging across to the next boat. The river split in two here, so you crossed twice.

Today a highway bridge carries the trucks over in seconds, and the wide stony banks have become a weekend picnic ground. Grandpa turns corn on the grill, his grandson waits with a plate, and Mom lays out rice balls by the open hatchback. Give the river Hiroshige's indigo and the corn a bright yellow.

Then Two ferries, one long pole, and a patient wait.

Now One bridge, a hot grill, and a patient grandson.

Hamamatsu

Winter at Hamamatsu, and nothing much to do but keep warm. Hiroshige gathers a few farmers around a fire at the foot of one enormous tree, its smoke climbing higher than the trunk itself. A traveler in a wide straw hat pauses to share the heat, and far beyond the bare fields sits the town with its castle.

Our fire is a dondo-yaki, the January bonfire where New Year decorations go up in smoke and children toast rice cakes on bamboo poles. The castle is still on the horizon, now joined by a tall city tower. Wrap everyone in bright winter colors and keep the smoke a soft gray.

Maisaka

At Maisaka the road simply ran out of land. Lake Hamana opened to the sea at Imagiri, a gap torn by an earthquake and tsunami in 1498, and travelers boarded boats for Arai. Hiroshige frames the water with a row of wooden stakes in the foreground and, if you look to the far right, a small white Mount Fuji.

Our stakes are still standing, but the boats can wait. When the tide drops, families wade into the shallows with rakes and buckets for clams, and sailboats drift by the steep green hill. Try a deep indigo for the lake and let the mud flats glow ochre.

Then Waiting on the shore for the boat across the lake.

Arai

The boat ride from Maisaka ended at Arai, home of a strict checkpoint whose building, remarkably, still stands. Hiroshige shows a lord's grand barge, its curtain marked with crests and its streamers snapping in the wind. In the plain little boat up front, the servants stretch and yawn. Spot the one with his arms flung high, plainly bored of the crossing.

The old ferry gave way to bridges, yet small boats now carry visitors across the water again. Our passengers wear life jackets, and a festive boat strung with pennants takes the barge's place. Color the flags vermilion and let the wind do the rest.

Then A lord's barge, crests flying, servants yawning.

Now Life jackets on, pennants up, everyone leaning out.

Shirasuka

Shiomizaka means "the slope for viewing the tide," and for travelers heading east from Kyoto this was the first glimpse of the open Pacific. Hiroshige frames the sea between two dark banks of pine. The view is just as generous today. A family walks the old road down, a pair of trucks hums along the highway below, and two cargo ships stand in for the far white sails. Give the sea a clear blue and save a little orange for the dawn sky.

Futagawa

Past Futagawa the road crossed a lonely, scrubby upland called Saru-ga-baba. Hiroshige sends three women musicians, called goze, trudging across it, their instruments bundled on their backs. On the left a little teahouse offers a rest, and its sign advertises the local treat, kashiwa mochi, rice cakes wrapped in oak leaves.

Our three are a school band heading home on a windy afternoon, an instrument case and a drum slung over their shoulders. The teahouse has become a small roadside shop with an awning, a banner, and a vending machine. Color their cases boldly; musicians never travel quietly for long.

Yoshida

Yoshida, today's city of Toyohashi, was a castle town on the Toyo River, and its long wooden bridge was famous in its own right. Hiroshige climbs up the castle with the repairmen. Our crew wears hard hats and safety harnesses, and the scaffold is steel, but the temptation is unchanged. One worker has paused to admire the view: a bus and a scooter crossing the river, the mountains beyond. Paint the harness straps bright, and forgive him the break.

Goyu

Goyu was famous for the persistence of its inn touts, women who hauled tired travelers off the road and toward their lodgings. Hiroshige plays it as slapstick. One tout grips a man by his bundle, another by his hat cord, and the poor fellow looks half strangled. Inside the inn on the right, a guest who surrendered earlier soaks his feet.

The old townhouses still line the street, but the touts have retired. Now the tugging is done by two children, steering a reluctant father toward a row of capsule toy machines. A wooden barrel nods to the town's miso. Color the prizes in every shade.

Akasaka

Akasaka sat only a short walk from Goyu, and its inns stayed busy into the night. Hiroshige slices one open like a dollhouse. A guest shuffles back from the bath with a towel over his shoulder, a maid carries in dinner trays, and next door the women of the inn are getting ready for the evening. Between the rooms rises a shaggy sago palm.

Our inn is now a guesthouse. The palm and the stone lantern stay; an air conditioner and a TV move in. Someone fresh from the bath, someone with a book, a backpack waiting by the lamp. Color the palm a deep verdigris green.

Fujikawa

The bobana was the marker at the edge of a post town, and Hiroshige stations us right beside Fujikawa's tall wooden post and stone embankment. A lord's procession is arriving, with horses wearing tufted paper ornaments, and local officials kneel in welcome. Look at the lower left: even a small dog seems to be bowing.

Fujikawa has revived a purple-eared barley praised in old poetry, and in spring the fields sway with it. Our hikers part ways at the same stone wall while a farmer pushes a wheelbarrow and a little train slides past. A wash of wisteria purple will bring the barley to life.

Okazaki

Okazaki was the castle town where Tokugawa Ieyasu, founder of the shogunate, was born, and travelers reached it over the Yahagi Bridge, one of the longest in Japan. Hiroshige lets the bridge sweep across the whole sheet on its wooden legs. Follow the procession over the planks, a long bead of hats and lacquered boxes, toward the castle roofs at the far right.

Today the Yahagi is crossed by a highway bridge with streetlamps and trucks. Our travelers are high school students biking to class in helmets, and the castle still keeps watch from the distance. Make the river a deep indigo and the morning sky pale gold.

Then A lord's procession files across the wooden bridge.

Chiryu

Each early summer Chiryu held a famous horse fair, and Hiroshige shows the meadow crowded with horses tied to little stakes, grazing while their owners attend to business. Find the lone pine in the middle: the dealers have gathered in its shade to haggle, and the deals are struck far from the animals themselves.

Horse fairs are long gone from Chiryu, but every morning the riverbank fills with four-legged company of another kind. Our walkers lead an assortment of dogs along a fence, the tall tree stands in for the pine, and a train hurries across the far bridge. Give every dog its own coat.

Narumi

Near Narumi lay the village of Arimatsu, and every traveler knew it for one thing: shibori, cloth tie-dyed into tiny rings and ripples. Hiroshige sets two women walking past a shop whose dyed fabrics hang in the window. One shades her face with a hat as a palanquin and a rider on horseback follow along the wide, quiet street.

The shops are still there, and so is the cloth. Arimatsu shibori is made to this day along a street of old merchant houses. Our shopper carries a bag past hanging banners, a mother pedals by, and a delivery man wheels his parcels. Try indigo and white for those rings.

Miya

Miya grew up around Atsuta Shrine, one of the most revered in Japan, and Hiroshige chose a festival night. Two teams of men race past a great torii, hauling a horse on ropes, while bonfires throw smoke into the dark. Notice the matching jackets: red-patterned for one team, blue for the other, a rivalry you can see at a glance.

Atsuta now stands in the middle of Nagoya, its towers rising over the trees. In our scene the horse becomes a festival drum cart, hauled under the torii by rope while a drummer stands on top. Lanterns and food stalls do the rest. Choose your team colors.

Kuwana

From Miya the Tōkaidō went to sea. The Seven-Ri Crossing took travelers across the bay by boat, and Kuwana was where they stepped ashore. Hiroshige brings two big ferries in under the castle walls, their huge sails being lowered as they glide toward the landing. Look at the stones: the castle rises straight out of the water.

A turret rebuilt in 2003 now stands near the old landing, and Kuwana's fishermen still bring in the clams the town has been proud of for centuries. Our boat carries crates of them, its small sail up at the stern. Color those clamshells in soft grays and creams.

Yokkaichi

Hiroshige's Yokkaichi is all wind. Yokkaichi is now a port city known for its great industrial complex, and our skyline bristles with smokestacks and cranes. The gale is the same. A cap and a water bottle tumble off, and someone on the bridge battles an umbrella turned inside out. Color the sky stormy gray.

Ishiyakushi

Shono

Kameyama

Snow is rare in Hiroshige's Tōkaidō, which makes Kameyama sparkle. The morning has cleared, the sky glows pink, and a lord's procession inches up an almost absurdly steep slope toward the castle-town gate. Tilt your head: the whole hillside runs on a diagonal, and the bearers' hats look ready to slide back down.

Kameyama Castle keeps an original turret on its high stone wall, the only surviving castle building in Mie Prefecture. Our climbers wear puffy coats and boots, a snowman keeps watch, and one child has wisely chosen the sled. Leave plenty of white, and touch the sky with pink.

Seki

Sakanoshita

Below the Suzuka Pass, travelers at Sakanoshita stopped at a teahouse to admire Fudesute, the "Brush-Throwing Mountain." Legend says the great painter Kano Motonobu tried to capture its crags, failed, and flung his brush away. Hiroshige took up the challenge anyway. Notice the man by the teahouse pointing out the view to his companion.

The highway still climbs past those rocks, a favorite route for trucks and touring motorcycles. Our riders have parked at a roadside rest stop with vending machines to stare up at the cliffs, just as the tea drinkers did. Whatever happens, keep hold of your pencils.

Tsuchiyama

An old packhorse song says the sun shines on the pass, Suzuka is cloudy, and at Tsuchiyama it rains. Hiroshige agreed. His procession crosses a bridge toward the woods of Tamura Shrine, every head bowed under a straw hat, while the swollen river roars beside them. The wooden bridge by the shrine was rebuilt in 2005, and the tall trees still stand. Our walkers are a line of schoolchildren in rain ponchos, dots, stars, stripes and flowers, following a grown-up across. Make every poncho a different bright color against the gray.

Minakuchi

Minakuchi's specialty was kanpyo, long ribbons shaved from a large gourd and dried in the sun. Hiroshige shows the village women at work beside a big tree. One kneels on a straw mat peeling the gourd, another lifts the strips, and the pale ribbons droop from poles like garlands. A traveler strides past without a glance.

Kanpyo is still made here and hung out to dry by hand, and in 2024 Minakuchi kanpyo won official protection as a regional product. Our grower lifts gourds from a crate as the strips sway on the pole, and a child dashes off with an insect cage. Keep the ribbons creamy white.

Ishibe

Near Ishibe stood the village of Megawa, whose teahouses were famous for tofu dengaku, grilled and brushed with sweet miso, served with rice cooked with greens. The dish has survived: dengaku is still served along the old road at Ishibe. Our view is from inside, through a wooden lattice, as a server carries the skewers and two joggers race the sunset outside. Vermilion for the sun, and something warm for the miso.

Kusatsu

Kusatsu was where two great roads met: the Tōkaidō and the inland Nakasendo joined here for the last stretch to Kyoto. Hiroshige parks us outside a teahouse selling ubagamochi, a small sweet rice cake, while two palanquin teams jog past in opposite directions. The shop sign hangs from the eaves, and the travelers inside are too busy eating to look up.

The junction is still marked by a tall stone signpost from 1816, and a tunnel nearby once carried the Nakasendo beneath an old riverbed. Ubagamochi is still sold in Kusatsu. Our two walkers part at the post, one road each. Color the stone a soft gray.

Otsu

Otsu was the last stop before Kyoto, near the Osaka Pass, and Hiroshige chose the Hashirii teahouse, named for its gushing spring. Find the water bubbling up from a stone basin at lower left, where a man fills his tubs. Meanwhile a line of ox carts loaded with rice bales lumbers past, each wheel nearly as tall as the driver.

Otsu still has a long, slow procession down the middle of its street: a four-car train that runs right on the road, sharing the lanes with cars before reaching the lake. A grandmother and child wait on the sidewalk to watch it glide past. The oxen would approve.

Kyoto

Here the Tōkaidō ends, at the Sanjo Bridge over the Kamo River. Hiroshige gives the capital a quiet welcome: the soft Higashiyama hills, the roofs of the city, and a stream of travelers crossing the long wooden span. A lord's palanquin moves among them, and someone under a parasol has, at last, nowhere left to hurry.

The bridge still marks the finish, its railings topped with rounded finials, and the character for "big" is cut into the hillside behind. Our travelers pause for the photograph every journey deserves. You have come all the way from Nihonbashi, fifty-three stations behind you. Color this one slowly.
